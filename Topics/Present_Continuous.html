Unit 1: Present Continuous

I am doing

The present continuous describes actions and situations that are in progress now, around the present time, or changing gradually.

1. Core Structure and Meaning

We use the present continuous when an activity is happening at or around the time of speaking. The action is viewed as being in progress, rather than completed.

Example situation

Sarah is in her car. She is on her way to work.

She's driving to work. (= She is driving to work.)

The action started before the moment of speaking and has not finished yet. The speaker focuses on the activity as something currently in progress.

am / is / are + verb-ing
Subject Auxiliary Verb Example
I am driving I'm driving.
he / she / it is working He's working.
we / you / they are doing You're doing it.

Questions and negatives

Form Example
Affirmative She is working.
Negative She isn't working.
Question Is she working?
Wh-question What is she doing?

Literary Example: An Activity Happening Now

Jerry Jean Webster
Read on Project Gutenberg
Context: A character is trying to understand why a young American man is still staying in Valedolmo. She asks what he is doing there.
‘By the way, Gustavo, how does it happen that that young American man who left last week is still here?’

Gustavo nearly dropped the eggs.

‘I just saw him in the garden with a book—I am sure it was the same young man. What is he doing all this time in Valedolmo?’
Grammar analysis: What is he doing asks about an activity that is happening or continuing around the present situation. The speaker uses is + doing because the activity is viewed as being in progress rather than as a habitual activity.

2. Actions in Progress Around Now

The present continuous is not limited to something happening at the exact second when you speak. It can describe an activity that is in progress during a period surrounding the present.

Compare:

“Where's Mark?”
“He's having a shower.”

Mark may not be visible to the speaker, but the activity is happening now.

Other natural examples include:

  • Please don't make so much noise. I'm trying to work.
  • Let's go out now. It isn't raining any more.
  • How's your new job? Are you enjoying it?
  • What's all that noise? What's going on?

The same form can describe a temporary activity taking place over a wider period:

  • I'm reading a really good book at the moment.
  • She's learning Italian.
  • Some friends of mine are building their own house.

Literary Example: An Activity in Progress

The New Tenants Elanora Kinsley Marble, in Birds Illustrated by Color Photography
Read on Project Gutenberg
Context: The poem describes a pair of wrens constructing a nest during the summer. The activities are presented as ongoing work.
Father and mother are building a nest;
They have found in Greenwood the place that is best.
They are working so hard through the long summer day,
Gathering grasses and hair and hay.
Grammar analysis: are building and are working describe activities that are currently in progress over a period of time. The birds are not necessarily performing every part of the activity at the exact second the reader sees the sentence; the focus is on an unfinished, ongoing process.

3. Periods Around Now

We can use the present continuous with expressions such as today, this week, this month, and this year.

These expressions refer to a period that includes the present moment. The speaker presents the situation as temporary or currently developing.

Examples:

You're working hard today.
The company I work for isn't doing so well this year.

Compare this with the present simple when the speaker is describing a regular or permanent situation:

Present continuous Present simple
I'm working hard this week. I work hard every week.
The company is doing well this year. The company does well every year.

Literary Example: An Ongoing Situation

How to Build a House Published by Project Gutenberg
Read on Project Gutenberg
Context: The speaker is discussing architectural construction and explains that the subject under discussion is a house currently being constructed.
“But just now, let us keep to our proper business. It is a house, not a temple or a basilica, that we are building. We have to consider all its parts; and this is work enough for us.

We have leisure to consider the details of our building thoroughly, since the frost obliges us to suspend the works. Construction, my dear fellow, is an art requiring foresight. The good builder is he who leaves nothing to chance, who does not put off the solution of any problem, and who knows how to give each function its place and value with respect to the whole, and that at the right moment.
Grammar analysis: we are building describes a project that is underway. The construction is not presented as a completed fact; it is an unfinished activity extending over a period of time.

4. Gradual Changes and Developments

The present continuous is also used when something is changing, developing, increasing, decreasing, or gradually becoming different.

Common verbs include:

  • get / become — getting better, becoming more difficult
  • start / begin — starting to change
  • change — changing rapidly
  • improve — improving steadily
  • increase / rise — increasing, rising
  • fall / decrease — falling, decreasing
  • grow — growing larger or greater
Examples:

Is your English getting better?
The population of the world is increasing very fast.
I'm starting to enjoy my job now.

The important idea is not simply that something exists now. The speaker sees a process of change taking place.

Literary Example: A Gradual Change

Anne of Green Gables Lucy Maud Montgomery
Read on Project Gutenberg
Context: Anne is describing her appearance and imagines that she is gradually becoming less thin. The change is presented as something developing over time.
There’s a piece of a plate with a spray of red and yellow ivy on it that is especially beautiful. We keep it in the parlor and we have the fairy glass there, too. The fairy glass is as lovely as a dream. Diana found it out in the woods behind their chicken house. It’s all full of rainbows—just little young rainbows that haven’t grown big yet— and Diana’s mother told her it was broken off a hanging lamp they once had. But it’s nice to imagine the fairies lost it one night when they had a ball, so we call it the fairy glass.

Matthew is going to make us a table. Oh, we have named that little round pool over in Mr. Barry’s field Willowmere. I got that name out of the book Diana lent me. That was a thrilling book, Marilla. The heroine had five lovers. I’d be satisfied with one, wouldn’t you? She was very handsome and she went through great tribulations. She could faint as easy as anything. I’d love to be able to faint, wouldn’t you, Marilla? It’s so romantic. But I’m really very healthy for all I’m so thin. I believe I’m getting fatter, though. Don’t you think I am?
Grammar analysis: I'm getting fatter describes a gradual change. Anne is not simply saying that she is fat; she is describing a developing change in her condition.

Literary Example: Something Gradually Changing

Little Women Louisa May Alcott
Read on Project Gutenberg
Context: Jo is outside during changing winter weather. The narrator describes how the length of the days is gradually changing.
Meg went back to toast her feet and read Ivanhoe, and Jo began to dig paths with great energy. The snow was light, and with her broom she soon swept a path all round the garden, for Beth to walk in when the sun came out and the invalid dolls needed air. Now, the garden separated the Marches’ house from that of Mr. Laurence. Both stood in a suburb of the city, which was still country-like, with groves and lawns, large gardens, and quiet streets. The days kept getting longer and longer, the weather was unusually variable and so were tempers; an unsettled feeling possessed everyone, and Satan found plenty of mischief for the idle hands to do.
Grammar note: This passage illustrates the same idea of gradual development, although the literary narrative uses the past form were rather than the present continuous because the story is being narrated in the past. The present-continuous equivalent would be: The days are getting longer and longer.

5. Present Continuous vs. Present Simple

The most important contrast in this unit is between an action viewed as temporary and in progress and an action viewed as regular, habitual, or generally true.

Feature Present continuous Present simple
Form am / is / are + -ing base verb / -s
Main meaning Activity in progress or temporary situation Habit, routine, fact, or general situation
Time relationship Now or around now Generally, repeatedly, or habitually
Focus The activity/process The fact, habit, or regular situation
Example I'm reading a good book at the moment. I read before bed every night.
Example She's learning Italian. She speaks Italian.
Useful question:

Ask yourself: Am I describing something in progress or developing around now, or am I describing something generally true or habitual?

6. Summary

Pattern Form Main use Example
Action now am/is/are + -ing Something happening at the time of speaking She's driving.
Activity around now am/is/are + -ing An unfinished temporary activity I'm reading a good book.
Current period am/is/are + -ing A temporary situation during today, this week, this year, etc. You're working hard this week.
Gradual change am/is/are + -ing A process of change or development My English is getting better.
Question Am/Is/Are + subject + -ing? Ask about an activity in progress What are you doing?
Negative am/is/are + not + -ing Say that an activity is not in progress It isn't raining.

Jerry — Complete Passage

The parrot, since she had last conversed with him, had acquired several new expressions in the English tongue. As Gustavo reappeared with the eggs, she confronted him sternly.

‘Have you been teaching this bird English? I am surprised!’

‘No, signorina. It was—it was——’ Gustavo mopped his brow. ‘He jus’ pick it up.’

‘I’m sorry that the Hotel du Lac has guests that use such language; it’s very shocking.’

‘Si, signorina.’

‘By the way, Gustavo, how does it happen that that young American man who left last week is still here?’

Gustavo nearly dropped the eggs.

‘I just saw him in the garden with a book—I am sure it was the same young man. What is he doing all this time in Valedolmo?’

Gustavo’s eyes roved wildly until they lighted on the tennis-court.

‘He—he stay, signorina, to play lawn-tennis wif me, but he go to-morrow.’

‘Oh, he is going to-morrow?—What’s his name, Gustavo?’

They are so happy, for soon they will hear

Dear mother, gather them safe ’neath each wing;
Kind father, hasten, for food you must bring.

Now mother and father will teach them to fly:
“Come, timid birdies; come, try; come, try.
Fly out in the Greenwood, dear birdies, with me;
Then back to the nest in the dear old tree.”

How to Build a House — Complete Passage

“But you will study these branches of architecture a little later on. I think it a bad method of teaching art to allow flowers to be introduced into discourse before the power of expressing thought clearly has been acquired; and it is thus that writers and speakers are formed who take balderdash for eloquence; and architects, who before they think of doing justice to the exigencies of construction, and studying the requirements of the case, amuse themselves with reproducing forms into whose origin, justification, and real meaning they have never inquired. But just now, let us keep to our proper business. It is a house, not a temple or a basilica, that we are building. We have to consider all its parts; and this is work enough for us.

“We have leisure to consider the details of our building thoroughly, since the frost obliges us to suspend the works. Construction, my dear fellow, is an art requiring foresight. The good builder is he who leaves nothing to chance, who does not put off the solution of any problem, and who knows how to give each function its place and value with respect to the whole, and that at the right moment. We have drawn plans for the several stories; we have given the details necessary for constructing the lower parts of the house; now we must draw the working elevations. The first thing is to make an exact section of the front walls giving the height of the floors, the levels of the tie-bars, and the base of the roof.”
