Unit 10

Present Perfect Continuous and Simple

(I have been doing and I have done)
1

Activity vs. Result (Painting the Bedroom)

We use the Present Perfect Continuous (have/has been + -ing) when we are interested in the activity itself. It does not matter whether the activity has been finished or not.

We use the Present Perfect Simple (have/has + past participle) when we are interested in the completed result. The action is finished, and we focus on what has been produced or achieved.
Example Situation:
• Kate’s clothes are covered in yellow paint. She has been painting her bedroom. (Focus on the activity—she was doing it recently).
• The bedroom was green. Now it is yellow. She has painted her bedroom. (Focus on the completed result—the painting is finished).

The child finds here none of the drudgery—to him quite meaningless—so often associated with the disagreeable task of learning how to read; the child reads from the very first page—he has been reading all through the Primer—reads with delight and real understanding...
Why this form? The verb has been reading is in the Present Perfect Continuous because it focuses on the child's ongoing experience and activity throughout the pages, rather than on finishing the book.
2

How Long vs. How Much / How Many

Use the Present Perfect Continuous to ask or state how long an action has been taking place (up to now).
Use the Present Perfect Simple to ask or state how much, how many, or how many times something has been completed.
Example Situation:
• Amy is writing emails. She has been writing emails all morning. (How long?)
• Amy has sent 40 emails this morning. (How many?)
How I Write My Novels
by Duchess (Margaret Wolfe Hungerford)
View on Project Gutenberg (#27621)
Context: The author describes her process of creative effort and writing over time versus finished literary outputs.
To sit down in cold blood and deliberately set to cudgel one's brains with a view to dragging from them a plot wherewith to make a book is (I have been told) the habit of some writers...
Why this form? have been told expresses a repeating or ongoing message given over time, focusing on the duration/experience rather than a specific counted occurrence.
3

Non-Continuous Verbs & Special Exceptions

Some verbs (e.g., know, have, like, believe, understand) are stative and are not normally used in continuous forms. We use the Present Perfect Simple with these verbs even when talking about duration.

Note exception: Verbs like want and mean CAN be used in the Present Perfect Continuous to emphasize continuous inner intention (e.g., I've been meaning to call you).
Example Situation:
• Correct: I have known about the problem for a long time. (Not I've been knowing).
• Correct: I have been meaning to write to you, but I was busy.

4

Comparison: Continuous vs. Simple

Feature Present Perfect Continuous Present Perfect Simple
Form have/has + been + verb-ing have/has + past participle
Main Focus The action/activity itself; ongoing or recent process The completed result or achievement
Key Question How long? How much? How many? How many times?
State of Action May or may not be finished Definitely completed
Example I have been reading that book for 3 hours. I have read 150 pages of that book.
5

Summary of Key Patterns

Pattern Form Main Use Example
Ongoing Activity have/has + been + -ing Focus on activity duration or recent action She has been studying all day.
Completed Action have/has + past participle Focus on completed result or quantity She has passed three exams.
Stative Verbs have/has + past participle States existing up to now (know, have, be) We have known them since 2010.

Catriona — Robert Louis Stevenson

You are still—as when first I saw, as when I last addressed you—in the venerable city which I must always think of as my home. And I have come so far; and the sights and thoughts of my youth pursue me; and I see like a vision the youth of my father, and of his father, and the whole stream of lives flowing down there far in the north, with the sound of laughter and tears, to cast me out in the end, as by a sudden freshet, on these ultimate shores.
